Section one

What we found.

First, what this is not. It is not a critique of your beer, your kitchen, your brand, or the people keeping the site current. Dark Horse has been pouring since 1997, is one of the last independents left in Michigan craft beer, runs a Mug Club people camp out for, and the story on your About page is the best writing on the site. The brand is not the problem, and neither is the copy. What follows is about the machine underneath it: how the site is built, what it weighs, what it tells search engines, and whose servers it runs on.

Nine findings, taken from your live site on September 3, 2026. Every one came off darkhorsebrewery.com itself and every one can be checked against it, most of them by tapping the link under it. Nothing below is an opinion, and nothing below needs our word for it.

A dinner menu you never wrote is live on your site

darkhorsebrewery.com/menu is titled “Dinner Menu | Dark Horse Brewing Co.” and lists Tuna sashimi at $4.50, Peanut crusted steak at $8.00, Schnitzel at $4.00, a “Healthy smoothie,” and Aperol Spritz, Gin & Tonic and Mojito at $1.50, over blurred stock photos of plates. It is the sample content Wix ships inside its restaurant-menu app, never replaced. It is not linked from your navigation, so nobody on staff sees it. But it is listed in restaurants-menu-sitemap.xml, which is the file you hand Google, and four more copies answer at /menu?menu=lunch-menu, breakfast-menu, drinks-menu and brunch-menu, titled “Menus (New).” Your real menu, the cheese bread and the brisket nachos and the wood-fired pizzas, is good. It lives as one section partway down a single long page called Brewery, mixed in with hours, events and the Mug Club, and it has no address of its own.

Order Carryout opened to a closed door

The most prominent button on every page of your site links to a Toast page whose address reads order.toasttab.com/online/roak-brewing-co-dark-horse-brewing-511-s-kalamazoo-ave. At 7:15 PM on Thursday, September 3, with the taproom open until 10 and the kitchen until 9 by the hours you post, that page said “Currently not accepting online orders.” No reason, no phone number in view, no other way to order. Whether Toast was switched off on purpose that evening or not, the visitor cannot tell, and your site offers nothing else to do. The same page shows your full pizza menu, your calzones and a Toast points program, none of which appear anywhere on darkhorsebrewery.com. And the address bar names ROAK, your Royal Oak brand, ahead of Dark Horse, on a link for the Marshall kitchen.

Live music is listed on the wrong day

On your homepage and your Events page, “Live Music Saturday: Boy Mob” is dated Mon, Sep 07. So is “Live Music Thursday: The Fat Animals.” Open the Boy Mob listing and it shows September 7, 6 to 9 PM, while the description underneath reads “Join us on Saturday, September 19th.” Three dates for one show. Your Instagram, which the homepage pulls in, says the series runs every Thursday and Saturday. September 7 is a Monday. Every listing also carries a “Buy Tickets” button that opens a checkout for a $0.00 ticket, so a customer asking whether they need one is put through a purchase to learn they do not.

The page people open on a phone is a 16.6 MB download

We ran Google’s own Lighthouse tool against your homepage on a phone profile. Total weight: 16,980 KB across 250 requests. The page becomes usable, by Google’s measure, after 42.7 seconds; the bar is under 4. 243 of the 250 requests go to servers that are not yours. The single biggest cause is the Instagram feed embedded at the bottom of the page: it loads eight Instagram video players and around sixty photographs before a visitor has scrolled to it, 11.4 MB on its own, and the largest single image on your homepage is a 1.4 MB Instagram photo shown at thumbnail size. The site sends nearly the same weight to a phone as to a desktop. Someone checking your hours from the parking lot on one bar of signal is downloading a feature film’s worth of Instagram to do it.

A 655 KB age check that never asks anyone’s age

Your homepage loads a script from age.bestfreecdn.com, a free age-verification widget. It weighs 655 KB, more than most entire websites, 567 KB of it never executed, and it blocks the page for a tenth of a second on every load. Across more than a dozen visits at two screen sizes, no age gate ever appeared. No prompt, no cookie, nothing in the page. You sell a 10 percent ABV malt beverage line and your site’s age check is a dead weight from a free-widget server, doing nothing but slowing the page down.

Google is told almost nothing about you

A page’s main heading is the first thing a search engine reads. Your homepage has two, and they are “Upcoming Events” and “JOIN OUR MAILING LIST.” Nothing on the page tells Google, in the form it reads, that this is a brewery in Marshall. Your About page, the one with the story, has no main heading at all. Your address, hours and phone number are on the site for humans but exist in no machine-readable form: there is no business, restaurant or brewery record in any page we checked for Google to build a listing or an hours panel from, only the generic “WebSite” stub Wix emits by default. The description Google is offered for your homepage is 402 characters; it shows about 155. And the icon in the browser tab, on every page, is Wix’s default icon, not your horse.

On a phone, your homepage headline is gone

On a desktop the intro over the hero video is headed “A NEW BREED.” Open the same page on a phone and the heading is not there. Not small, not hidden behind something: absent from the page. Wix keeps a separate, hand-arranged layout for phones, and this one was never finished, so most of your visitors go from video to paragraph with nothing to tell them what they are reading.

Open darkhorsebrewery.com on your phone and look for the words A New Breed

Your hours live in three places, and the Contact page has none of them

The homepage shows Taproom hours, opening at 11. Partway down the Brewery page, the Commons Market has its own hours and a line that matters more than anything on the homepage: breakfast Thursday to Sunday from 7 AM. Below that, the General Store has a third set. The one question people ask a brewery’s website most, “are you open right now,” has three answers on two pages and no logic behind any of them. Your Contact page, meanwhile, is a headline, an out-of-focus banner photograph and a form: no address, no phone number, no map, no hours. Both appear only in the small footer, below a blank gap half a screen tall, and the phone number there is plain text, so tapping it on a phone does nothing.

The small things a regular notices

Devil Dog, the flagship of your ROAK brand, is described as “An satmeal stout.” The About page says more than 5,000 Mug Club members; the Mug Club page says more than 4,800. The photographs on your About, Brewery and Work With Us pages carry camera filenames as their descriptions, IMG_0081.JPG, IMG_7844_edited.jpg, Untitled design (1).jpg, which is exactly what a screen reader announces. The Family of Brands page lives at /family-of-brands-1, the address Wix assigns when a page is created twice. The homepage calls you Dark Horse Brewing Company; every other page says Brewing Co. And the blog from your previous website still appears in Google, and now lands on a Page Not Found.

Some of these are a five-minute edit that nobody has made, which says something about how hard the platform makes it to see what is live. The rest, the weight, the headings, the missing business record, the tab icon, the vanished mobile headline, are what the platform does on every page, every time, regardless of who is typing.

Section two

The part that matters most.

Most businesses assume that paying for a website means owning it. On Wix, the paperwork is unusually clear that you do not.

What Wix says, in its own words

Wix’s help center, under “Exporting or Embedding Your Wix Site Elsewhere,” says it in one sentence: “Your site must run on Wix’s servers.” There is no export. There is no file you can take to another host. Stop paying, and the site stops existing; what you keep is whatever text somebody copies out by hand. Twenty-nine years of brand, and the website for it disappears the month a card payment fails.

Someone who wants your hours, your menu, your tap list, a pizza, a shirt and a mug renewal touches four companies and one person. None of the four is Dark Horse. And the Mug Club is the one that should keep you up at night: 4,800 people who line up in December, and the whole digital record of them is whatever is in one mailbox.

The one thing you do own

Your domain. darkhorsebrewery.com has been registered in your name through Network Solutions since February 2005, and it is paid up to 2029. That is the address everybody knows, the one on every can and every sign, and it is yours outright. Everything behind it is rented. Our whole proposal is to put something behind that address that belongs to you too.

What we would do instead

You own it. All of it.

When the build fee is paid, the site becomes yours in writing: design, source code, configuration, the Mug Club list. If you ever leave us you get the working project: repository, hosting, logins. Not a snapshot. Not an export. Month to month, thirty days’ notice, no exit fee. Your domain stays where it is, in your name. That clause is titled No hostage-taking, because we would rather say it plainly than bury it.

Three questions worth asking this week

Who has the Wix login?And the Toast admin, the Untappd for Business account, the InkSoft store, the Network Solutions account. If the answer to any of them is one person’s personal email, that is a risk today, before any decision about a website.
If Emily is out for a month, who can renew a mug?Not who could figure it out. Who can do it today, from the list as it exists, without her.
Where are the original photos and logo files?Every image on the site lives on Wix’s servers as a resized copy. Ask for the originals now, while it is easy.

What it costs.

One-time build. Design, development, the Mug Club system, content migration, launch. Half to start, half on launch.
$195/mo
Hosting, SSL, updates, security, backups, the workroom, and content edits whenever you would rather we made them.

That is $6,840 across the first twelve months, then $2,340 a year. Extra work is always quoted and approved before it starts, so nothing lands on your bill by surprise.

What the monthly replaces

We are not going to guess at your Wix bill. What we can say is what goes away: the Wix plan, the Wix Events fees on anything you do charge for, the free age widget that costs your visitors 655 KB a page, and the InkSoft store if you ever want it gone. Toast, if you keep it, stays what it is, and the Mug Club renewals move from a mailbox to a card on file.

Year one costs more than doing nothing. You are buying a website; that is a real number on top of a bill you already have, and any proposal that hides it is not worth reading. Year two, you own the result.

Worth saying plainly

We are a small shop in Marshall, the same town as your taproom, not an agency with a floor of account managers. That is why the price looks the way it does, and it is also why you get the person who builds the thing on the phone when something needs changing. If you want a vendor with a procurement portal, we are not that. If you want your website fixed and kept fixed by somebody down the road, we are exactly that.

Section six

What happens next.

1

Look at the demo

Ten minutes: darkhorse.glazedweb.com/demo. It was built before this proposal, on our own time. If it is not right, that is useful information and it costs you nothing to say so.

2

A conversation, not a pitch

Half an hour, at the taproom or on a call. Pick the time at glazedweb.com/schedule and it lands in both calendars. Two questions we would want to ask: who needs to approve a website change, and what does your team actually need to update week to week without calling anybody?

3

Find your Wix renewal date

Wix plans are usually prepaid for the year and renew on their own. Find the date before you decide anything, including deciding to stay. We will happily build toward it; there is no reason to pay two vendors at once.

4

Build

Roughly four to six weeks from a signed scope to launch. The variable is how fast menus, the Mug Club rules and photography come together on your side. The build itself runs to schedule.

What we would need from you

The taproom, pizza and breakfast menus in any format, the Mug Club rules and price as they actually work today, logo files and whatever photography you have, access to Untappd for Business and Toast, the Network Solutions login, and one person empowered to say yes. That is genuinely the list.
